Artifact Fidelity Is Not a Bonus

ATØR Institute · Deep Drift Research · 2026

A model can produce a persuasive answer and still fail the task. This distinction becomes obvious the moment generative systems are asked to make finished artifacts rather than merely talk about them.

Deep Drift defines artifact fidelity across six linked dimensions: semantic correctness, structural correctness, formatting fidelity, editability, export reliability, and provenance. A DOCX with correct prose but broken tables is not a successful document. A spreadsheet with the right visible numbers but damaged formulas is not a successful workbook. A generated file that cannot be traced back to the instructions and decisions that produced it is incomplete as a professional artifact.

This matters because contemporary systems increasingly claim the ability to operate workflows, create files, invoke tools, and return finished work. Once execution becomes part of the product promise, quality control must move beyond whether the language sounds intelligent.

The benchmark therefore changes from “Did the model answer?” to “Did the system execute, preserve constraints, produce the requested structure, and verify completion?” The difference sounds embarrassingly obvious. Software apparently needed generative AI to rediscover the concept of correct output.

Artifact fidelity is especially important for research, archives, conservation, design, finance, and institutional work, where one damaged structure can invalidate an otherwise plausible result.

Derived from Deep Drift workflow reliability research and ATØR Institute independent quality-control work.
